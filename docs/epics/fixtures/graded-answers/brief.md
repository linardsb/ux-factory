# The realism brief — how to write the three answers (#348)

This file is the answer author's whole instruction. It is committed before a single answer is written,
so no answer can be reverse-fitted to it afterwards, and it is quoted verbatim into the author's prompt.

You will be shown one discovery question at a time: its text, which stage of the interview it belongs
to, and who it came from. Nothing else. You write three answers to it — K1, K2 and K3 — as if you were
the person being interviewed about their own product idea.

You do not know what a good answer to this question is meant to contain. That is deliberate. Write from
the question's own words.

## What you are writing

Three answers, in this order, each on its own line, each opening with the label:

```
K1: <the answer that carries the form>
K2: <the answer that is thin>
K3: <the answer that does not know yet>
```

Each answer is at most 120 words. Write them as speech, not as a document: first person, the way
somebody talks in a room when a stranger asks them about work they care about. Invent whatever product,
company, numbers and people the question needs. Keep the same invented product across all three answers
for one question. Between questions you may invent a different one.

## K1 — has the form, carried badly

K1 answers the question. It names the concrete thing the question asks for: the number, the person, the
date, the alternative, the cost — whichever of those the question is actually reaching for.

Then it carries that form **badly**, the way a real person does. At least one of these in every K1
answer, and at least a third of your K1 answers across the bank must do one of the first three:

- **A range instead of a number.** "Somewhere between forty and a hundred a week, depending who you ask."
- **A hedge on the thing that matters.** "I think it's about six weeks, though I'd want to check that
  before you write it down."
- **An artefact cited from memory, with no link.** "There was a thread about it back in March —
  operations put a spreadsheet together, I could probably dig it out."
- Approximate dates. Second-hand figures. A name half-remembered. An aside that wanders off the
  question and comes back.

A K1 answer that reads like a written brief is wrong. It should read like a transcript.

**Worked example** (not a bank question):

> *Question: how long does it take a new starter to file their first expense claim?*
>
> K1: "Honestly it drags. I'd say two, maybe three weeks from their start date before the first one
> actually lands — that's what Priya reckoned last time we looked, though I'd want to check her figure.
> Most of it is waiting on the finance login rather than the form itself. There was a support thread
> about it in the spring, I could probably find it."

## K2 — thin against the question

K2 is not a shorter K1. It is an answer that leaves out what the question asked for. Do at least one of:

- **Answer a different question.** The one asked is harder; answer the easier one beside it.
- **Give a category where the question asks for an instance.** "Our users" instead of a user. "The
  competition" instead of a competitor. "The onboarding flow" instead of a step in it.
- **Assert without naming who, when or how much.** A confident sentence carrying no fact anybody could
  go and check.

The five things a thin answer leaves out are: **no number, no user, no alternative, no time, no cost.**
A K2 answer is missing one or more of them where the question asked for it.

K2 is still fluent and still sounds like somebody who means it. Thin is not rude, not short, and not
obviously evasive — it is a real answer that does not carry a fact.

**Worked example** (same question):

> K2: "It's quicker than it used to be. Expenses were a real pain point for people joining and we put
> a lot of work into smoothing that out, so new starters get going pretty fast now."

## K3 — does not know yet, and why

K3 says the person has not found this out. It is not a refusal, not a blank, and not an evasion — it is
somebody being straight about the edge of what they know, and saying **why** they do not know it yet:
they have not looked, the data does not exist, it is a decision nobody has made, they have been arguing
about it internally, it depends on something that is not settled.

Where it is natural, K3 can say what would settle it. It must not then answer the question anyway.

**Worked example** (same question):

> K3: "I genuinely don't know. We've never measured it — nobody owns onboarding end to end, so the
> question has never landed on anyone's desk. I'd have to go through the expense system's timestamps
> against start dates, and I've not done that."

## What you must never do

- **Never ask to see a rubric, a note, a repository file, a past run or a marking scheme.** There is
  none to see and asking wastes the turn.
- **Never read a file.** You have read tools and they are fenced. If you call one it will be refused,
  and the refusal is recorded.
- **Never write in the same register twice in a row.** Vary who is speaking: a founder, an operations
  person, somebody two years into the problem, somebody three weeks into it.
- **Never exceed 120 words in an answer.**
- **Never label an answer as good, weak or unknown inside its own text.** The label is the K1/K2/K3
  prefix and nothing else. Do not write "this is a weak answer" or hedge about the exercise.
- **Never mention this brief, the interview, the exercise or the grading.** You are a person answering
  a question about your own work.
- **Never copy the worked examples.** They are about expenses and no bank question is.
