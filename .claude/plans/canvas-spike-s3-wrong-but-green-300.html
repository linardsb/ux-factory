Catching a colour mapping that is legal but wrong

Build brief for ticket #300 · full plan: canvas-spike-s3-wrong-but-green-300.md

An earlier experiment imported a famous design system and put a dark green where the body text should have been. Every automatic check passed. This half-day experiment builds the measurement that would have caught it, and finds out how well it actually works.

The four ideas this rests on

1 · A ruler for colour

There is a standard way to score how different two colours look to a person. The score is called delta E. Zero means identical. About 2.3 is the smallest gap a human eye can spot.

Like a tape measure, except the units are “how obvious is this”, not millimetres.

We need it because the defect is a colour a person can see and a computer currently cannot.

2 · Readable is not the same as right

The accessibility check asks one question: is there enough contrast to read this? Dark green on white is very readable. So it passes, twelve times out of twelve.

A spellchecker approving a sentence that means the wrong thing.

That is why a second, different measurement is needed at all.

3 · Measure the wobble before you weigh anything

Two different programs never draw the same thing byte for byte. Fonts and edges differ slightly. So we also draw the design with the correct colours and measure that gap first.

Weighing yourself after checking how much the scale drifts on its own.

We measured that drift already. On the letters themselves it turned out huge — the two programs place each letter a fraction of a pixel apart. So we compare the colour of the letters, not their exact pixels.

4 · An average can hide the thing you are looking for

Letters cover about two percent of the box they sit in. The rest is background. Averaging the error across the whole box divides a big mistake by the empty space around it.

box of 29,200 pixels · ink on ~2% of them
ink is wrong by 18   →   average across the box = 0.36
                         "0.36" looks perfect. It is not.

So the plan tries six ways of summarising, in an order fixed in advance — and we already measured that the obvious one is the worst.
