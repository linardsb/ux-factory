#### seq 10 · `s6-accountable-when-wrong` — solution

> Legally, the food business operator, meaning the owner's company, is accountable today and stays accountable; our design does not move that and must not claim to. In practice the blame lands on the waiter who said "no nuts" and the chef who answered from memory, and the porter who signed the substitution is never in the frame. Our design does change the practical picture, and I want to say that plainly rather than discover it in a disciplinary. A ledger with a name and a time against every change makes blame precise: a chef who left a dish "unverified" for three days is now visible, and so is a waiter who read an unverified line and answered anyway. That precision is what the owner is buying and it is why the chef will not love it, which is the Q6 problem again. Three commitments follow. The system never says "safe"; it says what is recorded and who last confirmed it, and the chef's confirmation is the act of record. We are accountable for the record being faithful, meaning what was entered is what is shown and nothing is altered after the fact, not for whether the chef was right. And nobody, including the owner, can edit history; the moment the owner can tidy the ledger before an inspector sees it, it is worthless as evidence and becomes a blame tool instead.

#### seq 12 · `s8-eval` — solution

> There are three model steps and each gets its own eval: the delivery-note photo to a substitution line, the spec-sheet PDF to the fourteen flags plus may-contain, and the match from a supplier product name to a recipe ingredient. The set is ours, not a benchmark: two hundred spec sheets from our real suppliers including the scanned, multi-page, footnoted ones, the hundred or so delivery-note photos the capture test produces, and every product-to-ingredient pair on the flagship site's menu, each labelled independently by two head chefs so we also know how often the chefs disagree with each other, which is the ceiling on what "correct" can mean. The owner of the eval is the flagship head chef, because it is their judgement the system stands in for, and they run it, not the engineers. Pass rate is asymmetric. A missed "contains" is a diner; the pass bar is zero on the set, and one miss fails the release. A false positive costs a chef one confirmation, so up to five percent is acceptable. The match step passes at ninety-five percent with every unmatched line surfacing as "unverified" rather than dropped. It is run against the whole pipeline, not the model in isolation, re-run on every supplier change and every model change, and the number goes to the owner monthly, because it is their name on the record.

A false positive, a dish flagged for an allergen it does not contain, is visible within the hour because the chef sees it, and it costs one confirmation and a diner turned away from a dish they could have eaten; the price is trust, because a chef who sees three of those stops reading the flags. A false negative, a missed "contains" on a spec sheet, is invisible until a diner reacts, and there may be months between the two. A match failure, a substitution that reaches no dish, is invisible in the same way. An unreadable photo that nobody bounces back is a substitution that never happened as far as the ledger knows. So the design's job is to make the invisible ones visible: unmatched lines surface as unverified rather than disappearing, an unreadable photo goes straight back to the porter's phone, and every week a second chef re-checks a sample of dishes the system says are confirmed, which is the only check that can catch a missed "contains" before a diner does. Who pays for a wrong answer, in order: the diner with their body, the waiter and the chef with their jobs, the owner in court, and us in the witness box explaining the model version. The order never changes; the design only changes how long the queue is.
