#### seq 2 · `s1-how-addressed-today` — business

> Today the problem is handled by the merchant ERP, by periodic counting, by spreadsheets, and by the memory of experienced staff, and each of these covers a part of it while leaving the core gap open.
>
>   The ERP, whether Merchanter, Kerridge K8, Intact iQ or similar, is the system of record for sales, purchasing, and stock. It is good at recording what staff type in, and that is its limit. Stock levels are as accurate as the
>   last manual adjustment, and price lists change when someone loads a new one, so the system reports a version of the business that is days or weeks behind the yard. Most vendors now offer reporting and margin analysis, but
>   these show last month's margin after the money has gone.
>
>   Stock accuracy is patched with counts. An annual count closes the branch for a day or two and cycle counts consume a few staff days a month, and either way the numbers are stale the morning after. Barcode scanning on
>   handhelds helps for packaged goods but breaks down for timber that is cut, split from packs, or converted from cubic metres to linear metres, because the scanned item is no longer the item that was received.
>
>   Pricing is patched with spreadsheets. The buyer or managing director keeps cost-plus rules in a workbook and pushes a new price list quarterly, so every cost movement in between is absorbed as margin loss or as a manual
>   override at the counter that nobody reviews.

#### seq 19 · `s3-why-now` — business

> Parts of it have been built, and the reasons the whole has not are about economics and data rather than ideas.
>
>   The nationals solved it a decade ago with central pricing teams and bespoke systems, and retail inventory software has done live cost and cycle counting for years. What nobody built is the version for a fragmented market of a
>   few hundred independents, each with a different yard, timber that changes form, and an IT budget that stretches to one ERP subscription. The vendors serving that market, us included, made their money on implementation and
>   support, and a system that reports what was keyed in is cheaper to build and support than one that argues with the yard. The customer blamed themselves for stale prices, so there was no pressure on the vendor to fix it.
>
>   Three things have changed. Until recently, merchant ERPs were on-premise and the data was not reachable; the move to cloud over the last five years means we can read stock, cost, and sales continuously without a nightly
>   export. Supplier price files arrive as spreadsheets and PDFs by email, and the mapping to a merchant's own codes was a manual job that did not pay; document extraction with language models has made that cheap enough to do for
>   every supplier and every customer, which is the single technical change that makes the cost feed viable. And the yard hardware that used to cost thousands per device is now a rugged Android handset or the driver's own phone.
>
>   The demand side changed too. Before 2021 timber prices moved slowly and a quarterly list was fine. The volatility since then made stale pricing visible for the first time, and higher interest rates made the cash tied up in
>   excess stock a cost the owner can feel. The long-serving counter and yard staff whose memory patched the gap are retiring, and consolidators are buying the independents that cannot show a buyer a clean margin.
>
>   The honest counterpoint is that earlier attempts, dashboards and scanners sold as add-ons, did not fail for lack of demand. They failed on adoption, in the yard and at the counter, which is why the current plan spends its
>   risk on capture behaviour rather than on features.

#### seq 11 · `s6-accountable-when-wrong` — stakeholder

> Today accountability is diffuse, which is part of why the problem persists. When margin is short the managing director asks the branch manager, who points at the price list or the buyer, and individual counter staff are
>   blamed for individual sales only when a customer complains. When stock is wrong the foreman is asked where it went, and at year end the gap is written off as shrinkage, which is a way of saying nobody is accountable. The
>   buyer answers for over-ordering only when cash gets tight.
>
>   The design changes this in three ways, and not all of them are comfortable.
>
>   The margin guard turns an anonymous branch-level margin problem into a named override. Every sale below target now carries the name of the person who accepted the prompt and pressed on. That is the point of the feature, but
>   it moves accountability from the branch to the individual, and if the override report is used as a disciplinary tool rather than a coaching one, counter staff will find ways round it, most likely by keying the sale under
>   someone else's login or by pricing outside the system. Who sees the report, and how it is framed, is a design decision with accountability consequences.
>
>   The stock proposals create an owner for shrinkage. Someone approves each adjustment, and that person is now accountable for the stock figure in a way nobody was before. The MD will see that as a gain and the branch manager
>   will see it as exposure.

## MVP

#### seq 5 · `s1-what-would-have-to-be-true` — solution

> For Yardsense to work, a set of things about the yard, the software, the people, and the money all have to hold, and several of them are unproven.
>
>   On the yard, capture has to be cheap and nearly effortless. Stock truth depends on forklift scans, a driver app, and a weighbridge feed being used every time, by staff who today do not update the ERP until the next day. If
>   capture depends on new discipline rather than on fitting into what a forklift driver already does, the system has nothing to reconcile. It also has to be useful at partial accuracy. The yard will never be perfectly recorded,
>   so the product must deliver value at eighty per cent stock truth rather than requiring one hundred.
>
>   On the product model, timber that changes form has to be representable. A pack received in cubic metres, sold in linear metres, cut to length, with an offcut left over, must be traceable as one lineage of stock rather than as
>   unrelated items. If that model cannot be built and maintained by a merchant's own staff, every conversion becomes a support ticket.
>
>   On integration, the incumbent ERPs have to expose stock, cost, price, and sales data, and ideally accept writes back. Some merchant ERPs are closed or charge for access. Landed cost also has to be reconstructable, which means
>   haulage and surcharge invoices in the accounts package can be matched to the receipts they belong to.
>
>   On people, counter staff have to accept a price prompt without it slowing the sale, and the owner has to be willing to enforce it. Merchanting runs on relationships, so the margin guard has to allow the thirty-year customer
>   their discount while making its cost visible, rather than blocking it. Staff also have to trust the system's stock number over the foreman's memory, which only happens if it is visibly right early on.
>
>   On the money, the recovered margin has to be measurable and attributable to the product within a year, because independents do not buy on promise. If the leakage really is around one per cent of revenue, a subscription of one
>   to two thousand pounds a month per branch pays back quickly, and that ratio has to survive contact with real numbers.
>
>   On the market, the ERP vendors must either not ship this themselves within eighteen months or be willing to partner, and being independent of any one ERP has to matter to buyers. The single most uncertain assumption is the
>   first one, that yard capture can be made effortless, and it is the one discovery should test first.

#### seq 31 · `s8-failure-who-pays` — solution

> Failure is a wrong cost on the screen, and the two directions fail differently.
>
>   If the cost shown is too high, the salesperson prices up, the customer pushes back or walks, and the failure is visible within the minute. The merchant loses a sale and the salesperson loses face in front of a trade customer
>   who will mention it. If the cost shown is too low, the sale goes through at a loss, nobody notices, and the owner finds it at month end. That failure is invisible for weeks and it is the one that costs money, so the design
>   should be more afraid of confident-and-low than of high.
>
>   A prompt that fires when the margin is fine is a nuisance that is seen every time, and after a few dozen the salesperson stops reading. A prompt that fails to fire is silent, and a line that should have prompted and did not
>   is only found by replaying the quarter. A stale feed produces the worst combination: the prompt keeps firing, looks current, and is wrong on every sale of every line that supplier covers. The mitigation is that the prompt
>   shows the age of its cost, and that beyond a set number of days the prompt withdraws and shows "no live cost" rather than a number, which is a visible absence in place of an invisible error.

> The evidence is weak almost everywhere, and the plan is only defensible because it spends little before the evidence arrives.
>
>   The strongest thing we have is that the problem exists. The vendor pages you gave me say in their own words that counter prices do not reflect current costs and that stock is only as accurate as the last manual update, which

>   The frequency of failed deliveries, the four-day lag on price updates, the behaviour of counter staff under a prompt, the yard's tolerance for capture, and every willingness-to-pay figure are constructed, and the owner's
>   story about the Thursday in February is fiction I wrote. The technical feasibility claims rest on general knowledge that integrations and document extraction are routine, which is fair but untested against these supplier
>   files. The claim that a competitor will not ship first has nothing behind it at all.
>
>   Set against consequence, that is proportionate for the quick fixes, which cost days and cannot do harm, and it is not proportionate for anything else. A six-week cycle on the margin guard is a moderate bet, and the five owner
